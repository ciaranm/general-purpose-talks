\begin{frame}{The Slide That Keeps Getting Me Into Trouble}
    2021 \toolname{MiniZinc} challenge: for 1.28\% of instances, wrong solutions were claimed.
    \\
    \begin{itemize}
        \item False claims of unsatisfiability.
        \item False claims of optimality.
        \item Infeasible solutions produced.
        \item Not limited to a single solver, problem, or constraint.
        \item Not even consistent---same solver on same hardware and same instance can give
            different results on different runs.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Ludicrous Hypothesis: We Can Use \toolname{VeriPB}}
    \begin{block}{}
        A constraint programming solver can justify \alertblue{everything} it does, in a proof format
        that knows \alertblue{nothing} about constraint programming.
    \end{block}

    Four reasons this cannot possibly work:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            \alertblue{Can it say it?} The format has 0/1 variables and linear inequalities over
            them. We have integer variables, and a catalogue of several hundred global constraints.

        \item<3->
            \alertblue{Can it prove it?} Existing is not enough, proofs have to be short. The system
            SAT solvers reason in needs exponentially many steps to refute the pigeonhole principle.

        \item<4->
            \alertblue{Can we work it out?} A propagator is an algorithm rather than a deduction:
            matching, flow, dynamic programming, automata. Someone has to find the argument it is
            making.

        \item<5->
            \alertblue{Can we afford it?} Nobody leaves proof logging on if it costs a hundred times
            as much, or if the proof is too big to check.
    \end{itemize}

    \smallskip

    \uncover<6->{It mostly works. One format, one checker, and adding a constraint to your solver
    adds nothing to what you have to trust.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Really Important Idea}
    Solve exactly as normal, using the full power of constraint programming propagators etc.

    \medskip

    Also provide a simple pseudo-Boolean \alertblue{encoding} of the problem, just for proof logging.

    \medskip

    Justify the reasoning carried out against the encoding.

    \bigskip

    Critically, the solver itself does not do any reasoning about pseudo-Boolean things (unless it wants
    to\ldots). It just prints out lightly formatted text strings.
\end{frame}

\section{Encoding CP Variables}

\begin{frame}{Pseudo-Boolean Notation Recap}
    A \alertblue{pseudo-Boolean constraint} is an integer linear inequality over Booleans,
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_i c_i \ell_i \ge d
    \end{equation*}
    where a \alertblue{literal} $\ell_i$ is a variable $x$ or its negation $\overline{x} = 1 - x$,
    and a Boolean is a $1$ or a $0$ that we may do arithmetic with.

    \medskip

    Throughout: upper case $X$, $Y$ for constraint programming variables, lower case $x$, $y$ for
    pseudo-Boolean literals.

    \medskip

    A clause is the special case where every coefficient is one, and coefficients let us count:
    \begin{equation*}
        x \vee \overline{y} \vee z
        \quad\text{is}\quad
        x + \overline{y} + z \ge 1
        \qquad
        \text{at most one of them}
        \quad\text{is}\quad
        \overline{x} + \overline{y} + \overline{z} \ge 2
    \end{equation*}

    \toolname{VeriPB} \alertblue{normalises} everything to a $\ge$ with positive coefficients: a
    $\le$ is negated, an $=$ becomes two, and $-c\,x$ becomes $c\,\overline{x} - c$. It shows you
    what it stored, not what you wrote:
    \begin{equation*}
        1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 4 x_{b2} \le 4
        \quad\text{is}\quad
        1 \overline{x}_{b0} + 2 \overline{x}_{b1} + 4 \overline{x}_{b2} \ge 3
    \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudo-Boolean Reification Recap}
    \begin{equation*}
        b \Rightarrow \sum_i c_i x_i \ge d
        \qquad\text{becomes}\qquad
        M \overline{b} + \sum_i c_i x_i \ge d
    \end{equation*}
    for an $M$ big enough that the constraint says nothing at all when $b$ is false. Once everything
    is normalised, the smallest $M$ that works is the degree $d$.

    \medskip

    \toolname{VeriPB} writes it for you: \texttt{b ==> \ldots} and \texttt{b <== \ldots} normalise to
    precisely that constraint. Several literals on the left of \texttt{==>} is a conjunction, each
    contributing its own $M \overline{b}_i$, and $\Leftrightarrow$ is just both directions.

    \bigskip

    This is why we may write down things that are not literally pseudo-Boolean constraints:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            $x \Leftrightarrow y \wedge z$, because a conjunction \alertblue{is} a pseudo-Boolean
            constraint, $y + z \ge 2$. Reify it in both directions and we are done.
        \item
            $f \Rightarrow X < Y$, because once $X$ and $Y$ are encoded, $X < Y$ is a pseudo-Boolean
            constraint too. Reifying it is the same rewrite again.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The Direct Encoding}
    An encoding has to \alertblue{mean} what the variable means, and it has to provide every literal a
    propagator might want to talk about.
    It does \alertred{not} have to propagate well: this is only for checking, not solving.

    \medskip

    For $X \in \{0 \ldots 8\}$, one PB variable per value, with $x_{=v}$ meaning $X = v$:
    \begin{equation*}
        x_{=0} + x_{=1} + x_{=2} + x_{=3} + x_{=4} + x_{=5} + x_{=6} + x_{=7} + x_{=8} = 1
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item[\textcolor{uofglawn}{\ding{51}}]
            This is what a solver thinks a variable is. A domain is a set of literals, and deleting a
            value is falsifying one.
        \item[\textcolor{uofgpillarbox}{\ding{55}}]
            One literal per value. A variable over $\{0 \ldots 10^6\}$ is a million literals.
            Your solver might store this internally just as two integers.
        \item[\textcolor{uofgpillarbox}{\ding{55}}]
            Arithmetic is clumsy. $X$ itself is $\sum_v v \cdot x_{=v}$, so adding up three
            variables needs a term for every value of every one of them.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Order Encoding}
    One literal per value again, but $x_{\ge v}$ meaning $X \ge v$. Nothing says so yet, so we say it:
    \begin{equation*}
        x_{\ge 2} \Rightarrow x_{\ge 1}, \qquad x_{\ge 3} \Rightarrow x_{\ge 2}, \qquad \ldots
        \qquad x_{\ge 8} \Rightarrow x_{\ge 7}
    \end{equation*}
    each of which is the pseudo-Boolean constraint $x_{\ge v} + \overline{x}_{\ge v + 1} \ge 1$.

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item[\textcolor{uofglawn}{\ding{51}}]
            A bound is one literal. Most propagators reason about bounds, and now they have
            something to reason with.
        \item[\textcolor{uofglawn}{\ding{51}}]
            The direct literals come back for free, since $X = v$ is a bound from each side:
            \begin{equation*}
                x_{=v} \Leftrightarrow x_{\ge v} \wedge \overline{x}_{\ge v + 1}
            \end{equation*}
        \item[\textcolor{uofgpillarbox}{\ding{55}}]
            Still one literal per value, and arithmetic is still clumsy.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bits Encoding}
    Say what the variable \alertblue{is}, in binary, and introduce no literals for values at all:
    \begin{equation*}
        X = 1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 4 x_{b2} + 8 x_{b3}
    \end{equation*}
    with extra constraints bounding $X$:
    \begin{align*}
        1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 4 x_{b2} + 8 x_{b3} \ge 0 \\
        1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 4 x_{b2} + 8 x_{b3} \le 8
    \end{align*}

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item[\textcolor{uofglawn}{\ding{51}}]
            Logarithmic. A million values is twenty literals.
        \item[\textcolor{uofglawn}{\ding{51}}]
            Arithmetic is free. $X + Y - Z \le 4$ \emph{is} a pseudo-Boolean constraint once the
            bits are substituted in: one constraint, not one constraint per value.
        \item[\textcolor{uofgpillarbox}{\ding{55}}]
            There is nothing to say. No literal means $X \ge 4$, or $X \ne 3$, and those are exactly
            what a propagator infers.
    \end{itemize}

    {\scriptsize Negative domains want a sign bit, with coefficient $-2^n$. Not appearing in
    this talk for simplicity.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hybrid Encodings}
    Have all three, and say how they relate:
    \begin{align*}
        x_{\ge v} &\Leftrightarrow 1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 4 x_{b2} + 8 x_{b3} \ge v \\
        x_{=v} &\Leftrightarrow x_{\ge v} \wedge \overline{x}_{\ge v + 1}
    \end{align*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            The \alertblue{bits} say what the variable is, and carry every linear constraint.
        \item
            The \alertblue{order} and \alertblue{direct} literals are used for propagator inferences.
        \item
            Each of these is a \alertblue{definition}. It says nothing about the problem, only about
            what the literals mean, so it is always available and always true.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lazy Variable Encodings}
    For serious solvers, we need something both scalable and powerful. We can get the best of all
    worlds:

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            Keep the \alertblue{bits} in the model, and introduce $x_{\ge v}$ or $x_{=v}$
            the first time a propagator wants to say something about $v$, in the \alertblue{proof}
            rather than in the model.
        \item
            Use \alertblue{extension variables}. In \toolname{VeriPB} this is the \proofrule{red} command.
        \item
            Each new literal is also linked to the neighbours it turns out to have, handing the checker
            $x_{\ge v} \Rightarrow x_{\ge j}$ for the closest $j < v$ that exists. Again, this is
            in the proof, not the definition.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Lazy Encoding?}
    All this effort goes on integer variables, so that propagators and search become easy to write.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            \alertblue{It stays small.} A \texttt{var int} in \toolname{MiniZinc} has astronomical bounds.
            A direct or an order encoding would not survive the declaration.

        \item<3->
            \alertblue{The layers stay in step.} Anything said in one is mirrored into the others by
            unit propagation alone: fix some bits and the order and direct literals follow; assert an
            order literal and the bits follow, and so does everything that bound implies.

        \item<4->
            So a propagator says what it means in whichever layer suits it, and nothing has to be
            maintained: the order chain is already unit propagation, and ``takes at least one
            value'' is a line of cutting planes rather than an axiom.

        \item<5->
            This is a \alertblue{pseudo-Boolean} property, not a Boolean one. One constraint compares
            a bit vector against a number, and propagates the moment a coefficient exceeds the
            slack. This is a big problem for CNF-based solvers.
    \end{itemize}

    \uncover<6->{\small Stated properly in Matthew McIlree's PhD thesis, chapter 3. If anyone is
    really good at Lean and wants to try formalising this, please talk to me.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The \toolname{Glasgow Constraint Solver}}
    A fairly serious conventional CP solver, performance-competitive on certain classes of problems.
    C++, around forty constraints, and every inference every one of them makes is justified.

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            Bits in the model, order and direct literals introduced lazily in the proof, exactly as on
            the previous slide.
        \item
            A more-or-less full set of constraints: all-different, table, regular, element, circuit,
            cumulative, knapsack, and so on.
        \item
            Optimisation and enumeration as well as satisfaction, so the proof has to justify the
            bounds and solutions too, and not only the failures.
    \end{itemize}

    \medskip

    {\centering\small\url{https://github.com/ciaranm/glasgow-constraint-solver}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\toolname{kindling}: a Vibe-Coded Solver, with Proof Logging, for Teaching}
    Under two thousand lines of Python, with no dependencies. Non-negative bounded integer
    variables, three constraints, depth-first search by cloning, no learning, and unsatisfiable
    instances only. Deliberately unpolished.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            Uses the hybrid encoding, written out in full for every variable before
            the search starts, no laziness.
        \item
            Written entirely by Claude---design, proofs, tests---and it steals heavily
            from the \toolname{Glasgow Constraint Solver}.
    \end{itemize}

    \medskip

\section{Encoding CP Constraints}

\begin{frame}{Linear Inequalities}
    Substitute the bits, and there is nothing left to do. For $X, Y, Z \in \{0 \ldots 7\}$, the
    constraint $2X + 3Y - Z \le 12$ is
    \begin{align*}
        2x_{b0} + 4x_{b1} + 8x_{b2}
        + 3y_{b0} + 6y_{b1} + 12y_{b2}
        - z_{b0} - 2z_{b1} - 4z_{b2} \le 12
    \end{align*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            One PB constraint, whatever the coefficients are, and its size depends upon how many variables
            there are rather than upon how big their domains are.
        \item
            It propagates almost nothing, which is still fine.
        \item
            \toolname{kindling} refuses any coefficient but $\pm 1$. Putting the coefficients back is one of
            the exercises.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Equals and Not-Equals}
    $X = Y$ is two inequalities, and we have just done inequalities:
    \begin{equation*}
        X \le Y \qquad\text{and}\qquad X \ge Y
    \end{equation*}

    \medskip

    $X \ne Y$ is not an inequality at all. So introduce a flag $f$ saying which way round the two
    variables go, and half-reify an inequality for each way:
    \begin{align*}
        f &\Rightarrow X < Y\\
        \overline{f} &\Rightarrow X > Y
    \end{align*}
    Either way round is allowed, but $f$ has to pick one, and neither of them is $X = Y$.

    For $X, Y \in \{0 \ldots 3\}$, that is two pseudo-Boolean constraints:
    \begin{align*}
        4 \overline{f} + 1 \overline{x}_{b0} + 2 \overline{x}_{b1} + 1 y_{b0} + 2 y_{b1} &\ge 4\\
        4 f + 1 x_{b0} + 2 x_{b1} + 1 \overline{y}_{b0} + 2 \overline{y}_{b1} &\ge 4
    \end{align*}
    where the $4$ is the degree: one for the strict $<$, three from the flipped signs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Table Constraints}
    For $(X, Y, Z) \in [(1, 2, 3), (1, 3, 4), (2, 2, 5)]$:
    \begin{align*}
        &t_1 + t_2 + t_3 \ge 1 \\
        &t_1 \Rightarrow x_{=1} \wedge y_{=2} \wedge z_{=3} \\
        &t_2 \Rightarrow x_{=1} \wedge y_{=3} \wedge z_{=4} \\
        &t_3 \Rightarrow x_{=2} \wedge y_{=2} \wedge z_{=5}
    \end{align*}

    \bigskip

    The tuple Booleans are proof-only. They don't (necessarily) exist inside the solver.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{All-Different (\toolname{kindling} Version)}
    One at-most-one constraint per \alertblue{value}, over the direct literals:
    \begin{equation*}
        \text{for each value } v: \qquad x_{1=v} + x_{2=v} + \cdots + x_{n=v} \le 1
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            Every value gets a constraint.
        \item
            The size is the number of variables times the size of the domain, which is precisely
            what the last section was about avoiding.
        \item
            Easier for the exercises, though\ldots
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{All-Different (\toolname{Glasgow} Version)}
    A clique of not-equals instead. One flag per pair, saying which way round that pair goes, and
    two half-reified constraints over the \alertblue{bits}:
    \begin{align*}
        s_{ij} &\Rightarrow X_i < X_j\\
        \overline{s}_{ij} &\Rightarrow X_i > X_j
    \end{align*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs, two constraints each, and not one mention of a value anywhere. This does
            not grow with the domain, so an all-different over unbounded integers costs no more than
            one over $\{1 \ldots n\}$.
        \item
            The at-most-one constraints over values have gone. They are \alertblue{consequences}
            of this encoding rather than part of it.
        \item
            Can derive them in the proof, once, the first time a propagator needs one.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recovering At-Most-One}
    \toolname{Glasgow} has no at-most-one constraint to add up: its all-different is a clique of not-equals. So
    we need to derive them.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            One pair first. Assume both take $v$: the two half-reified constraints for that pair become
            unit on its selector, one each way, and contradict. So $\overline{a} + \overline{b} \ge
            1$ is derivable by \proofrule{rup}.
    \end{itemize}

    \uncover<3->{\vspace*{-0.5em}
    \begin{align*}
        (\overline{a} + \overline{b} \ge 1) + (\overline{a} + \overline{c} \ge 1)
            &\quad=\quad 2\overline{a} + \overline{b} + \overline{c} \ge 2 \\
        +\ (\overline{b} + \overline{c} \ge 1)
            &\quad=\quad 2\overline{a} + 2\overline{b} + 2\overline{c} \ge 3 \\
        \div\ 2 &\quad=\quad \overline{a} + \overline{b} + \overline{c} \ge 2
    \end{align*}}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<4->
            Division \alertblue{rounds up}, so $3/2$ becomes $2$.
        \item<5->
            It generalises to $n$ literals as one \proofrule{pol} of $O(n^2)$ steps. Can then run the
            previous slide's argument.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reasons, Explanations, and Lazy Clause Generation}
    Every inference kindling logs is guarded by \alertblue{every guess} made on the way to the node.
    Always legal, because between them the guesses determine the whole node---and often more
    than was needed.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            A real solver could state a \alertblue{reason}: the handful of literals the propagator actually
            looked at. On \texttt{too-small}, the same refutation costs $4558$ literals with guesses
            and $2895$ with reasons.
        \item<3->
            That clause is the same object a lazy clause generation solver has to produce to explain
            a propagation.
        \item<4->
            So a solver that already does lazy clause generation is most of the way to logging
            proofs.
        \item<5->
            There is one catch: a reason can be \alertred{wrong}. This will make a proof fail, but
            can be hard to debug\ldots
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Else Can We Do?}
    \toolname{Glasgow} has thirty-seven constraints, and every inference every one of them makes is justified:

    \smallskip

    {\small\raggedright
    Abs, AllDifferent, AllEqual, Among, AtMostOne, BinPacking, Circuit, Comparison, Count,
    Cumulative, Diffn, Disjunctive, DivMod, Element, Equals, GCC, In, Increasing, Inverse, Knapsack,
    Lex, Linear, Logical, MDD, MinDistance, MinMax, Multiply, NValue, Parity, PlusMinus, Power,
    Regular, SeqPrecedeChain, SmartTable, Sort, Table, ValuePrecede.\par}

    \medskip

    Also reformulations, each of which infers something the model never said, and
    \alertblue{derives} it rather than asserting it:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            Auto-tabulation: notice that a subproblem is small, solve it, and replace it by a table.
        \item<3->
            Capacity strengthening by integrality, conflict cliques across resources, lifted cover
            cuts: three ways of telling a scheduling problem something about itself that none of its
            constraints says.
        \item<4->
            Difference logic: spot that a pile of two-term inequalities is a precedence network, and
            hand it to a temporal propagator.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{It Is the Same Handful of Tricks}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            \alertblue{Search you did not have to do.} A table propagator finds an unsupported value
            efficiently, not by trying it. The justification says ``had you guessed this, it would
            not have worked``.
        \item
            \alertblue{Case analysis.} Enumerate what is left and knock it over. Free: it is
            what \proofrule{rup} does.
        \item
            \alertblue{Linear arithmetic.} Add constraints, scale them, round them off. Linear, Abs, and
            every bound that has to cross between the bits and the order literals.
        \item
            \alertblue{Counting.} Pigeonhole, and recovering an at-most-one from pairwise
            conflicts. All-different's Hall sets, GCC, NValue, Among.
        \item
            \alertblue{Naming things.} Introduce a flag standing for a condition and argue about
            that instead. Reification, cumulative's before and after flags, smart tables.
        \item
            \alertblue{Dynamic programming.} A flag per state, an implication per transition:
            replay the computation rather than arguing about its answer. Regular, knapsack,
            decision diagrams.
        \item
            \alertblue{Orderings and reachability.} A rank witness, or a transitive closure. Sort,
            Inverse, Circuit.
        \item
            \alertblue{Flow arguments.} Theoretically possible, unclear if they're practical yet.
    \end{itemize}

    \uncover<2->{Every propagator on the last slide needs two or three of these put together
    cleverly.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Doable, But Not Pretty}
    Multiplication, division and modulus are all certified. Nobody enjoyed it.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            A product is awkward in \alertblue{both} views at once. The bits do not add up into
            anything the order literals can see, and a bound on a product is not a bound on anything
            linear, so neither of the two things that rescue everything else is available.
        \item<3->
            What comes out is bit-level case analysis and a long, specific derivation per
            propagation rule, rather than one recipe you could explain in a slide. This one took a
            chapter.
        \item<4->
            We do not know whether that ugliness is \alertblue{inherent}. No lower bound says a
            short certificate cannot exist; nobody has found one.
        \item<5->
            Which is where every one of these has started. Parity looked impossible until 2021, and
            then it was four ingredients and only one of them was hard.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Other Approaches: Assert Everything, Up Front}
    Everybody working on this has the same problem---a propagator knows something the checker does
    not---and differs only over who is trusted to close the gap. Both of the alternatives here
    belong to people who are in this room, so they can tell you where the description is unfair.

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            Gange, Chu and Stuckey, \emph{Certifying Optimality in Constraint Programming}: take a
            lazy clause generation solver, write out the resolution proof it already had, and
            introduce each propagator's explanations as \alertblue{axioms} saying what that
            propagator does.
        \item<3->
            A certified inference checker per constraint then rules on whether those axioms were
            allowed.
        \item<4->
            In our vocabulary: put every \texttt{a} into the model file and leave it there. Then
            wonder why the checker ran out of memory.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Other Approaches: Assert Everything, Then Trim}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1->
            DRCP, and the Pumpkin solver: Flippo, Sidorov, Marijnissen, Smits and Demirovi\'c, CP
            2024. At runtime the solver logs only a \alertblue{scaffold}: what it did, in terms
            of literal constraints on domains, and not why.
        \item<2->
            Afterwards a proof processor \alertblue{trims} the scaffold, throwing away everything the
            answer turned out not to depend upon, and expands what is left into something a checker
            will take.
        \item<3->
            In our vocabulary: assert the lot, cheaply, and work out afterwards which of those
            assertions you actually have to pay for.
        \item<4->
            Somewhere, something still has to know what a \texttt{cumulative} is. We put it in the
            solver's derivation, where a checker that has never heard of scheduling can read it.
            They put it between the solver and the checker. Which you prefer depends on what you
            think happens when somebody invents a new global constraint.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stealing the Best Bits}
    The right answer is probably all three at once. This one is Matthew McIlree's, written down in
    the conclusion of his thesis and nowhere else, and he is building it inside Huub: a lazy clause
    generation solver which is not ours either.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            The solver emits \texttt{a} lines and nothing else, but fills in the \alertblue{reason}
            and the hints, in the annotation fields we have spent all afternoon leaving blank.
        \item<3->
            \alertblue{Trim first.} Elaborate the skeleton, throw away every inference the conclusion
            turned out not to need, and only then start working out justifications.
        \item<4->
            Justify \alertblue{outside} the solver. Every derivation in this talk needs only the
            reason, plus occasionally something small like an automaton's state, so a justifier can
            do it afterwards rather than every solver doing it for itself. His is lifted from the
            \toolname{Glasgow} solver.
        \item<5->
            And when nothing in the library fits: fall back on a decomposition, an LP relaxation, or
            handing the goal to a proof logging pseudo-Boolean or SAT solver to find the argument
            for you.
    \end{itemize}

    \uncover<6->{What is missing is a production quality trimmer for \toolname{VeriPB}. Which is, officially,
    what I am currently paying Matthew to work on.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open Problems: The Other Four Hundred Propagators}
    The joke used to be that we had done six constraints, and the global constraint catalogue had
    about four hundred.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            That is getting harder to say with a straight face. Scheduling was the large hole, and
            it is mostly filled: time-tabling, edge finding, energetic reasoning, not-first and
            not-last, over variable durations, demands and capacities.
        \item<3->
            None of those needed anything added to the proof vocabulary. What they needed was
            somebody to sit down and work out the argument, which is a different kind of problem
            from an open one.
        \item<4->
            The techniques generalise. Anything that can be written as a smart table or MDD is
            already justifiable, which quietly covers a good fraction of the catalogue without
            anyone doing it one at a time.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open Problems: Everything That Is Not an Integer}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1->
            \alertblue{Set variables.} Can represent sets by lots of Booleans, but solvers have more
            efficient alternatives.
        \item<2->
            \alertblue{Unbounded integers.} Our encoding opens by asking how many bits a variable
            needs, and a variable with no bounds has no answer.
        \item<3->
            \alertblue{Floating point.} Even the semantics are hairy.
        \item<4->
            \alertblue{Strings.} Again, needs a size bound, which can be hard to trust.
    \end{itemize}

    \uncover<5->{The pattern is that we have proof logging for the parts of constraint programming
    that look like integers, and that is not all of constraint programming.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open Problems: The Gap at the Top}
    Remember the second slide, and the arrow labelled \emph{verified input}.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            We can check the proof. We can also verify that the pseudo-Boolean model says the same
            thing as the solver's own input format.
        \item<3->
            Nobody writes their model in the solver's own input format. They write
            \toolname{MiniZinc}, and
            something flattens it.
        \item<4->
            A flattener is a \alertblue{compiler}. It reformulates, it decomposes, it introduces
            variables, it is thousands of lines long, and it has not been verified.
        \item<5->
            So today, \enquote{verified} honestly means \emph{verified from the point at which we
            started paying attention}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Biggest Open Problem}
    Everything on the last three slides is work. Somebody has to do it, and it will get done.

    \medskip

    \uncover<2->{This one is not work. It is an argument.}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<3->
            A solver says \emph{unsatisfiable}, and we believe it. It says \emph{optimal}, and we
            put that number in a paper, or a timetable, or a hospital roster.
        \item<4->
            The SAT community settled this over a decade ago. If you want to enter the competition,
            your solver produces a proof, and nobody argues about it any more.
        \item<5->
            Constraint programming has not settled it. Neither has almost anything else in
            computer science.
    \end{itemize}

    \medskip

    \uncover<6->{You are the people who will write the next generation of solvers. Please make them
    show their working.}
\end{frame}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Installing \toolname{kindling}}
    \begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
git clone https://github.com/ciaranm/kindling.git
cd kindling
python3 -m kindling pigeonhole --prove /tmp/ph --check
python3 -m unittest discover -s tests -t .
    \end{Verbatim}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            Nothing to install. Python's standard library, and the \toolname{VeriPB} you already have.
        \item<3->
            On a fresh clone the only failures are in \texttt{tests/test\_exercises.py}, one per
            exercise, each naming what is still unjustified. If anything \alertblue{else} fails you
            have broken the solver rather than not finished it.
        \item<4->
            The worksheet is \texttt{docs/practical.md}, and it has more detail than these slides.
            The answers are on the \texttt{ash} branch---there is nothing to cheat at, so go and
            look if you are stuck.
        \item<5->
            One switch worth knowing before you start: \texttt{-{}-no-justifications} logs the
            search and leaves out everything the propagators worked out. Try it on
            \texttt{agreement} and then on \texttt{tight-sum}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{\toolname{VeriPB}'s Brand New Experimental Interactive Mode}
    Type a line, and find out what it did:

    \begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small,commandchars=\\\{\}]
pbp> pol @lin1 @x2ge3_up + @x3ge3_up + @x1ge4_up + s ;
pbp> :show x1ge4
pbp> :undo
\alertred{(line rejected, state unchanged)}
    \end{Verbatim}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            \texttt{:show} lists the constraint database, filtered by variable or by number.
            \texttt{:list} prints what you have typed so far, \texttt{:undo} takes it back, and
            \texttt{:save} writes the session out as a real \texttt{.pbp}.
        \item<3->
            A line that does not check is rejected and the state is left alone, so you can guess and
            keep guessing. Which is a different activity from editing Python, rerunning the solver,
            and reading a rejection at line 349.
        \item<4->
            It is genuinely brand new and it is on a branch. If it is not behaving on the day, then
            \texttt{-{}-trace-failed} and \texttt{-{}-trace-pol} do a coarser version of the same
            job.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Exercise 1: Table}
    \texttt{table\_int.py} removes a value when no tuple can support it any more, and asserts that
    it was allowed to. It was allowed to, and the reason is reverse unit propagation.

    \medskip

    Replace the two \texttt{Assert(...)} with \texttt{Rup()} and make \texttt{two-tables} verify.

    \medskip

    \uncover<2->{That is one word, so spend the time you saved on this. Try exactly the same thing
    in \texttt{int\_lin\_le}: replace its \texttt{Pol(...)} with \texttt{Rup()}, and run}

    \begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
python3 -m kindling tight-sum --prove /tmp/t --check
python3 -m kindling too-small --prove /tmp/t --check
    \end{Verbatim}

    \uncover<3->{One of those still verifies and the other does not. Work out why before you move
    on: it is the reason the next two exercises are harder than this one, and it is a property of
    how integers are encoded rather than anything about tables or sums.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Exercise 2: Negative Coefficients}
    \texttt{int\_lin\_le} justifies a bound with cutting planes: the constraint saying what the constraint
    is, plus one channelling constraint per term. Every bit cancels, and what is left is the clause.

    \medskip

    The reference handles positive coefficients. A negative one needs the same sum with
    \alertblue{one thing} changed, because a term that counts downwards needs its variable bounded
    from the other side. Look at \texttt{channelling}, and at the two constraints every variable has:

    \begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
@x1ge3_up 4 x1b2 2 x1b1 1 x1b0 3 ~x1ge3 >= 3 ;
@x1ge3_dn 4 ~x1b2 2 ~x1b1 1 ~x1b0 5 x1ge3 >= 5 ;
    \end{Verbatim}

    Make \texttt{over-budget} verify.

    \medskip

    \uncover<2->{Getting this wrong gives you \texttt{REJECTED} rather than
    \texttt{UNDER ASSERTIONS}, so you will know.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Exercise 3: Hall Sets}
    \texttt{all\_different\_int} never removes a value---it only fails, when it finds a set of
    variables with fewer values between them than there are variables. That argument is not unit
    propagation. It needs adding constraints up.

    \medskip

    Write \texttt{hall\_steps}, and swap the \texttt{Assert} for
    \texttt{Pol(self.hall\_steps(variables, values))}. The constraints you have:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item
            \texttt{@amo\{c\}\_\{v\}}: for each value, at most one of these variables takes it.
        \item
            \texttt{@atleast\{i\}}: each variable takes at least one value. Derived in the
            preamble rather than asserted in the \texttt{.opb}, because it is a consequence of what
            the literals mean rather than something the model says.
    \end{itemize}

    Make \texttt{pigeonhole} and \texttt{squeeze} verify.

    \medskip

    \uncover<2->{You will also want \texttt{w}, which weakens a constraint by dropping a term. Work out
    what needs dropping, and why the checker accepts the proof without it anyway.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Exercise 4: Debugging}
    Now the point of all of it. \texttt{-{}-bug} breaks a propagator on purpose:

    \begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
python3 -m kindling puzzle --bug table-forgets-a-tuple --prove /tmp/p --check
    \end{Verbatim}

    Run all four bugs against \texttt{puzzle}, \texttt{latin}, \texttt{tight-sum} and
    \texttt{over-budget}, and fill in what happens. Some of it is not what you would guess.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item<2->
            Three of them make the solver \alertred{lose the only solution} and report that there is
            none. No test of the answer alone would have caught that, on an instance whose answer
            you did not already know.
        \item<3->
            One leaves the solver completely correct. Right answer, every time, and only the
            derivation is wrong. Nothing you could write a test for is broken. The proof still
            fails.
        \item<4->
            And the one worth arguing about: run an unsound bug against an instance that really has
            no solution. The proof \alertblue{verifies}. Work out why that is the correct behaviour
            rather than a hole.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
